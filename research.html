Scholarship

Research

I work at the intersection of computational linguistics, natural language processing, and forensic stylistics — investigating how language models encode authorial identity and how that encoding can be operationalised for attribution, verification, and linguistic measurement.

Doctoral Work

PhD Thesis — University of Birmingham, 2022–2026

Style in the Eye of Machine: Authorial Attribution via Large Language Models

My thesis develops a framework called Authorial Language Models (ALMs) — author-specific fine-tunes of GPT-2 that encode an individual’s stylistic fingerprint in the model weights. The central claim is that the perplexity assigned by an ALM to an unseen text is a reliable, interpretable measure of stylistic distance between that text and the author’s known writings.

The framework is applied across three tasks: (1) closed-set authorship attribution, (2) open-set authorship verification including AI-generated text detection, and (3) relative lexical frequency estimation as a proxy for linguistic productivity. The work bridges classical stylometry with modern LLM methodology, offering both practical tools for forensic linguistics and theoretical contributions to the sociolinguistics of language modelling.

Focus Areas

Research Threads

Five interconnected threads span my work, each asking a variant of the same question: what can the internal representations of language models reveal about human language?

01

Authorship Attribution & Stylometry

Classic stylometry identifies authors from measurable surface features — function word frequencies, punctuation patterns, sentence length. My work replaces hand-crafted features with a single learned quantity: the perplexity of an author-specific language model. An ALM trained on an author’s known writing assigns low perplexity to their texts and high perplexity to others’ — encoding style holistically rather than through pre-selected features.

02

AI-Generated Text Detection

ChatGPT and similar systems leave measurable stylometric traces that distinguish them from human authors. With colleagues, I have shown that ALMs can detect AI-generated text by treating the AI itself as a candidate “author” whose stylistic profile is learnable. The open-set formulation — where the true author may not appear in the training set — maps directly onto real-world verification tasks.

03

LLM Perplexity as Linguistic Measurement

Beyond authorship, perplexity from a general-purpose LLM is a theoretically motivated proxy for relative lexical frequency: rare words yield higher surprisal than common ones. I am developing a probabilistic framework that calibrates LLM perplexity scores against corpus-based frequency counts, validating the measure on the British National Corpus and exploring its use in forensic vocabulary analysis where corpora are unavailable.

04

Metaphor Identification with LLMs

In collaboration with Jeanette Littlemore’s group at Birmingham, I am comparing three LLM-based approaches to automated metaphor identification: retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), zero- and few-shot prompt engineering, and supervised fine-tuning. The project uses the VU Amsterdam Metaphor Corpus and a custom domain corpus to benchmark methods against human annotation, with the goal of a scalable pipeline for metaphor extraction in clinical and educational contexts.

05

Corpus Linguistics & Sociolinguistic Variation

My broader computational linguistics work spans two strands: (i) the sociolinguistic underpinnings of language model training data — how register, dialect, and author demographics shape what LLMs “know” — and (ii) phylogenetic and corpus-based approaches to understudied language varieties such as Tanka, a maritime Cantonese variety, and code-switching patterns in Cantonese-English bilingual children.

Sociolinguistics Corpus methods Phylogenetics Code-switching BEAST2

Key paper: Frontiers in AI 2025 · Methods in Dialectology 2024

Sociolinguistic dimensions of LM training data

Register bias: web-scraped corpora over-represent formal, edited prose
Dialect gaps: minority and regional varieties remain underrepresented
Author demographics: age, gender, and L1 affect lexical and syntactic choices encoded in LMs
Implication: sociolinguistic awareness is necessary for fair, interpretable LMs
